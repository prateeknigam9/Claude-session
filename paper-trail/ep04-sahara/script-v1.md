# Case No. 04: Three Crore Names (Sahara) — script v1 (style v2), 2026-11-10

- **Status:** SCRIPT, waiting for your approval. Next comes the storyboard with sound, part by part.
- **The question we hold back:** were the three crore investors real? It has never been settled, so the episode doesn't settle it either. It lays out both sides and ends on what is still waiting in the account.
- **Style v2:**
  - Story-led. We follow three threads: a depositor (an example), the regulator's team opening the cartons (a reconstruction), and the founder (a generic puppet).
  - Each part works as question → reveal → choice.
  - Staged scenes, very little on-screen text.
  - Chatterbox voice; sound kept sparse.
- **Length:** about 1,700 narrated words, ≈ 12 minutes at the expressive pace, plus chapter cards and the end screen.
- **Voice moods:** `(low)` `(warm)` `(build)` `(hit)` set Chatterbox's intensity per line. `[pause]` means a real pause, and `[SCREEN: …]` is the picture.
- **Legal standing (stricter than Case 03):**
  - Nobody in this story has been convicted of fraud in the bond case. The Supreme Court ruled the money was raised **in breach of the law** and ordered it returned. That is the only finding stated as fact.
  - Subrata Roy was **sent to jail by the Supreme Court for not complying with its orders**, not for a conviction. He died in November 2023.
  - "Possibly fictitious investors" is always **the regulator's** finding. Sahara's answer ("not even one fictitious investor"; "we already paid them back") is always given next to it.
  - The enforcement agency's 2025–26 claims are **allegations**.
  - Words we don't use as fact: *scam*, *fraud*, *Ponzi*, *fake*. They can appear only as someone's attributed claim.

## COLD OPEN: 127 Trucks

[SCREEN: night. A Mumbai street in the rain. Headlights, one pair after another, all the way down the road.]

(low) January, twenty thirteen. Mumbai.
Outside the offices of India's market regulator, the trucks start to arrive.
Not one or two. A hundred and twenty-seven of them.

[SCREEN: a truck's back door rolls up. Cardboard cartons, floor to roof.]

(warm) Inside, thirty-one thousand cartons.
And inside the cartons, paper. Application forms. Receipts. Names.
About three crore names.

[SCREEN: a single form slides out of a box. A name, a village, a thumbprint. The face is never shown.]

(build) Every name is supposed to be a person who handed over their savings to one company.
And together, those savings come to more than twenty-four thousand crore rupees. Billions of dollars.

(low) The regulator has one job now.
Find these people. Give them their money back. [pause]

(hit) There's just one problem. [pause]

(low) Some of them may not be there to find.

[SCREEN: an envelope comes back across a desk, stamped. A second. A tenth. A pile.]

(build) What follows would take one of India's most famous men from a palace… to a prison… and leave a fortune sitting in an account, waiting for people nobody can find.

This is the paper trail. Case four. Three Crore Names.

## PART 1: THE FAMILY

[SCREEN: chapter card. CASE FILE 1 / 4 · THE FAMILY.]

(low) To understand the trucks, you have to start with a scooter.
Gorakhpur, nineteen seventy-eight.

[SCREEN: a dusty street. A young man on an old scooter, a cloth bag on his shoulder. DRAMATIZATION.]

(warm) A young man named Subrata Roy starts a small savings business. The stories say he had two thousand rupees, a borrowed scooter, and one desk.

His idea is simple.
Millions of Indians have no bank account. They earn a little every day, in cash.
So Sahara sends people to them.

[SCREEN: a lane at dawn. A market stall. A woman weighs onions. EXAMPLE · NOT A REAL PERSON.]

(low) Meet Kamla. She's our example: not a real person, but someone like millions who were.
She sells vegetables. Every evening, a man from Sahara stops at her stall.
Twenty rupees today. Thirty tomorrow. He writes it in a little book.

(warm) He knows her name. He knows her children's names.
He's not a banker in a tie. He's from the neighbourhood.
And he tells her she's part of something bigger. A family.

[SCREEN: a crowd of paper figures in matching uniforms, hands on hearts.]

(build) Sahara calls itself exactly that: the Sahara India Pariwar. The Sahara family.
And the family grows. And grows.

[SCREEN: the scooter becomes a car, then a plane, then a skyline; each one slides in faster.]

(build) An airline. Television channels. Townships. A private hill city outside Pune.
The name goes on the shirts of the Indian cricket team.
Hotels in London and New York.

(warm) Subrata Roy becomes "Saharasri". He lives like a king, and he says the family comes first.

[SCREEN: a long banquet table. At its head, a generic puppet in white, face turned away. A crown of paper flowers on the table.]

(low) And for years, nobody asks the simple question.
Where does all this money come from?

(low) Kamla doesn't ask either. Why would she?
Her book says her money is there. The man comes every evening.

[pause]

(low) But in a different office, far from her stall, someone is about to read the fine print.

## PART 2: THE LETTER

[SCREEN: chapter card. CASE FILE 2 / 4 · THE LETTER.]

(low) September, twenty oh-nine.
One of Sahara's companies wants to sell shares on the stock market.
To do that, it has to file a thick document with the regulator. Every detail. Every risk.

[SCREEN: a heavy document lands on a desk. A paper hand turns the pages, slowly.]

(warm) It's the kind of paperwork almost nobody reads.
But somewhere inside, it mentions two other Sahara companies.
And it says they've been raising money from the public.

[SCREEN: the hand stops. A finger rests on one line. The page has no readable text, just the line glowing.]

(low) A few weeks later, the complaints arrive.
One comes from a group of investor advocates.
Another is a letter, written in Hindi, from a man in Indore.

[SCREEN: an envelope with a hand-written Hindi address, a postmark from Indore. It is passed from one desk to another.]

(build) The man had written to the national housing bank. They passed it to the market regulator.
His question is short:
How are these companies collecting so much money from ordinary people… and who gave them permission?

[pause]

(low) The regulator starts asking.
And Sahara's answer is just as short: this is none of your business.

(warm) According to Sahara, these weren't shares or public bonds. They were a private offer. Something you make to friends, to family.

(hit) A private offer… to about three crore people.

[SCREEN: a tiny table with two chairs. The camera pulls back; the room fills with chairs, then the city, then the country.]

(build) The regulator doesn't accept that. In twenty eleven, it orders the two companies to pay everyone back, with interest.
Sahara appeals. It loses.
Then it appeals again, to the Supreme Court.

[SCREEN: the Supreme Court as a paper building, columns of folded card. Rain on the steps.]

(low) August, twenty twelve.
The court rules that this was a public offer all along, and that the money was raised in breach of the law.
Pay it back, it says. All of it. Fifteen percent interest.

(build) For the regulator, it feels like the end.
They just have to find the investors.
So they ask Sahara for the list.

[pause]

(hit) And Sahara sends it.

## PART 3: THE CARTONS

[SCREEN: chapter card. CASE FILE 3 / 4 · THE CARTONS.]

(low) This is where the trucks come in.

[SCREEN: the rain-soaked street again, the trucks in a line. This time we watch from the regulator's window. DRAMATIZATION.]

(warm) A hundred and twenty-seven of them. Thirty-one thousand cartons.
The regulator has to find a whole new building just to store them.
Then a team opens the first box.

[SCREEN: a warehouse of cartons, floor to ceiling. Two generic staff puppets with box cutters. A single bulb.]

(low) Application forms. Redemption slips. Millions and millions of them, mixed together.
Six hundred people will spend a year going through it all.

(build) And Sahara has a second surprise.
It says it has already paid most of these investors back. Directly. In cash.
So the regulator, it says, is chasing debts that are already settled.

[pause]

(low) The regulator starts sending letters, to check.

[SCREEN: a mail sorting room. Envelopes go out in sacks.]

(low) In one batch of twenty thousand letters, more than eight thousand come back.
The stamp from the post office: addressee untraceable.

[SCREEN: the stamp comes down. Again. Again.]

(build) Some addresses are just the name of a village. Some are just a highway.
The regulator tells the court it has found a huge number of possibly fictitious investors.

(warm) Sahara is furious. It says there cannot be even one fictitious investor. Its workers, it says, know every single one.

(low) Two stories. The same paper.
Either millions of real people already have their money back…
Or millions of names lead nowhere.

[SCREEN: one carton in the middle of the floor, lid half open. The bulb flickers.]

(low) And still, the court wants its money.

(build) February, twenty fourteen.
Subrata Roy doesn't appear when the Supreme Court calls him.
The court orders him detained.

[SCREEN: a generic puppet in white, walking between two officers. Camera flashes. His face stays turned away.]

(hit) Within days, one of the most famous men in India is in Tihar jail.
Not convicted of a crime. Held, the judges say, until he comes up with a concrete plan to pay.

(low) His people set him up in a room inside the jail, so he can try to sell his hotels.
London. New York. Buyers come and go.
The deals keep falling through.

[SCREEN: a bare room. A table, a phone, a stack of folders. Outside the barred window, a paper skyline: two hotels, slowly fading.]

(low) He stays for two years.
Then, in May twenty sixteen, his mother dies, and he is let out on parole for her funeral.

[pause]

(hit) He never goes back.

## PART 4: THE ACCOUNT

[SCREEN: chapter card. CASE FILE 4 / 4 · THE ACCOUNT.]

(low) Over the years, Sahara does pay money in. Not to the investors. To the regulator.
It goes into a special account. And there it sits, earning interest.

[SCREEN: a paper bank vault. A ledger page; a number climbs, slowly.]

(warm) The regulator puts ads in newspapers across the country. If you invested, come forward, show your papers, get your money.

(low) And the people… mostly don't come.

[SCREEN: an empty counter with a bell. Nobody rings it. The clock on the wall moves.]

(build) After ten years, the regulator has paid out about a hundred and thirty-eight crore rupees.
The account, by then, holds more than twenty-four thousand crore.

[pause]

(low) Sahara says that proves its point. The investors were real, and they were paid long ago.
The regulator says it proves the opposite.
Nobody has ever proved who is right.

[SCREEN: back at the market. Evening. Kamla's stall. EXAMPLE · NOT A REAL PERSON.]

(warm) And Kamla?
Here's the twist. Kamla was never in those two companies.
She put her money in Sahara's cooperative societies. A different scheme. A different line of paperwork.
And people like her, it turns out, are very real. Millions of them.

(low) For years, their passbooks say the money is there. Getting it out is another story.

(build) In twenty twenty-three, the government opens a website for them. And the Supreme Court lets some of the money in the regulator's account go to them instead.
Waiting for investors nobody could find… to pay the ones who were still waiting in line.

[SCREEN: a phone screen glows in the dark stall. A form. A green tick. A queue of paper figures behind her, stretching into the night.]

(low) By early twenty twenty-six, that website has paid out nearly nine thousand crore rupees to about forty lakh people.
Many more are still in line.

[pause]

(low) November, twenty twenty-three.
Subrata Roy dies in a Mumbai hospital. He was seventy-five.

[SCREEN: a white flower on a dark table. The banquet table from Part 1, empty.]

(build) The cases don't die with him.
In twenty twenty-five, India's financial crime agency arrests two men from the group's inner circle. It alleges that property bought with depositors' money was quietly sold off for cash.
And in March twenty twenty-six, a tribunal upholds the regulator's order once more: pay back the bond money.
Sahara's answer hasn't changed. Almost all of it, it says, was paid back years ago.

[SCREEN: the warehouse again. The cartons, still stacked. Dust in the light.]

(low) So somewhere, there are still a hundred and twenty-seven truckloads of paper.
Three crore names.
And a question that nobody has ever answered.

[pause]

(warm) Kamla trusted a man who knew her name.
That's how this kind of money moves: person to person, faith to faith.

(hit) So before you hand over your savings, don't ask who's promising. Ask who's checking. [pause]

(low) Because in this story, by the time anyone checked… the names were all that was left.

## Legal and style notes

- **Both sides, every time:** each regulator finding (untraceable, possibly fictitious) is followed by Sahara's answer in the same beat.
- **Roy:** generic puppet in white, face turned away, never a likeness. His jailing is described as the court's order for non-compliance, with "not convicted of a crime" said out loud.
- **The man in Indore:** a real complainant, not named in the narration. The script shows only his envelope.
- **Reconstructions:** the regulator's warehouse, the jail room and Kamla's stall carry the DRAMATIZATION tag (Kamla also carries EXAMPLE · NOT A REAL PERSON).
- **No logos:** SAHARA appears only as plain type. No cricket kit logos, no hotel brand marks.
- **Numbers kept on screen:** 127 trucks, three crore names, ₹138 crore against ₹24,000 crore. Everything else stays in the narration or out.
- **The takeaway:** one line, "Don't ask who's promising. Ask who's checking."
